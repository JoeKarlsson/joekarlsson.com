This is [Pilgrim at Tinker Creek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pilgrim_at_Tinker_Creek) if Annie Dillard were stuck on a New York City sidewalk.

I mean that as the highest praise. I'm a sucker for sprawling slice-of-life documentaries, and I'll sit and watch them all day long. Just show me hyper-specific people living their weird little lives, and I'll be seated for it. I'm also a huge fan of [How To with John Wilson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/How_To_with_John_Wilson), and of anything Nathan Fielder touches (he executive produced the show and pops up here), and I was not disappointed by this at all.

![Nathan Fielder in a black hoodie and shorts walks away from a tall, wobbly tower of pillows stacked next to a hotel bed.](/images/reviews/the-history-of-concrete/nathan-fielder-stacking-pillows.webp)

The History of Concrete is one of the best films I've ever seen about the artistic process, because it shows how meandering creativity is. Noticing, paying attention and letting the journey take as long as it takes lead to the biggest creative and personal insights.

The behind-the-scenes of making it is the film. Wilson opens at a workshop on how to write and sell a Hallmark movie, then tries to use the formula to sell a documentary about concrete.

![A workshop instructor points up at a TV showing a slide titled "What does that look like?" with four Hallmark rules: Kindness, Escapism, Love interests and Inspirational.](/images/reviews/the-history-of-concrete/hallmark-workshop-rules-slide.webp)

The formula never stands a chance. In classic John Wilson fashion, he follows his instincts, meets people, and goes deeper and deeper into their lives, and every one of those detours ends up being the movie. He shows his work, Zoom pitches that go nowhere included, and it's the clearest look I've had at how an idea actually comes together.

The standout for me is the GumBusters guy, who has the Sisyphean task of removing gum from New York City sidewalks.

![A steam wand with a red collar blasts a blob of gum off a gray sidewalk, with more dark gum spots scattered across the same slab.](/images/reviews/the-history-of-concrete/gumbusters-steaming-gum-off-the-sidewalk.webp)

Even in the shot where he steams one blob away, there are more waiting on the same slab. The city will never run out of gum, and he seems joyful doing it anyway. Truly a character from a [Camus novel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Myth_of_Sisyphus).

![The GumBusters owner smiling in a gray GumBusters T-shirt in front of his company van.](/images/reviews/the-history-of-concrete/gumbusters-and-titians-sisyphus-1.webp 'The History of Concrete · 2026') ![Titian's painting of Sisyphus hunched under a huge boulder.](/images/reviews/the-history-of-concrete/gumbusters-and-titians-sisyphus-2.webp 'Titian, Sisyphus · 1548–49')

_The GumBusters owner, and Titian's Sisyphus (1548–49), Museo del Prado. Painting: Titian, [public domain](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Titian_-_Sisyphus_-_Madrid_-_Prado.jpg)._

He might also be the most John Wilson person in the whole movie, happily paying attention to the thing everyone else steps on.

Dillard's book has a whole chapter called "Seeing." She went to a beautiful slice of nature in Virginia and tried to get every ounce of insight out of a creek. Wilson is stuck in the concrete jungle doing the same thing with the stuff under his feet. Concrete is so easily passed over and misunderstood, and it turns out to be fertile ground. Somehow he and Annie Dillard cross paths, from such different angles.
