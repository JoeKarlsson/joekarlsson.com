This is what a biopic should be: not the facts of a life but the fever of one.

I don't need a beat-for-beat march through an artist's years. I want the feeling of it, the heat coming off it, and Jarman gives you exactly that. Every frame is composed like one of Caravaggio's own paintings, all candlelight and shadow, less a biography than a baroque canvas that happens to move.

The typewriter, the pocket calculator, the motorbike, the electric bar lights, and, crucially, the fact that Caravaggio himself dressed his biblical figures in the contemporary clothes of his own day. He dragged sacred time into the now. Jarman just runs the same play four centuries later. The modern junk doesn't break the spell, it's how he tells a truer thing about the man than any correct date could.

Ecstatic truth over factual truth, every time.

And god, it's gay. Properly, unapologetically, 1986-and-does-not-care gay, which you almost never got then and barely get now. The whole thing is Caravaggio on his deathbed remembering the two people he wanted, the street thug Ranuccio and Ranuccio's mistress Lena, a bisexual triangle where desire, class, art, and violence are all the same hunger. More of this. More bi men on screen who want everyone in the room. More, more, more.

It floors me that this was pulled off in a London studio for pocket change and still looks like the Louvre bleeding.

A dying man, a room full of everyone he desired, and the light doing all the talking. That's a life. Facts can sit this one out.

Stray thought: Swinton is so young here I stopped the film to check the credits. Her debut, the very start of her and Jarman, and she already has the face that gives absolutely nothing away.
