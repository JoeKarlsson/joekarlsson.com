In high school I wanted to draw comic books. Then I started college in 2008, in the middle of the Great Recession, picked the stable job, and spent most of my career writing code instead. Julia Cameron's _The Artist's Way_ is the first thing in years that got me listening to that kid again.

![A hand holding a yellow paperback of The Artist's Way by Julia Cameron, 30th anniversary edition.](/images/blog/cognitive-case-for-play-in-technical-work/my-copy-of-the-artists-way.webp 'margin: My copy, the 30th anniversary edition.')

I just finished all [12 weeks](https://juliacameronlive.com/the-artists-way-video-course/ "cite: Julia Cameron, The Artist's Way video course"). The morning pages and artist dates worked on me. Dreaming bigger didn't, and that's the part I keep thinking about.

## How I stopped making art

College is where I started cutting off the artist in me. I minored in math, and I held onto an art history minor because it kept me connected to something.

Tech then was optimistic and all about the hustle and the grind. In my experience it rewarded people who were insanely good at one narrow thing, not people with balanced, creative lives, and in my humble opinion that has hurt the whole field. I played the part. I got good at programming and stayed focused. I also stopped listening to and trusting myself, and I felt disconnected.

Even the things I made for fun didn't feel like art. I had some success with short videos on TikTok and Instagram, but it felt like performing and teaching. I still hate video editing. Making videos makes me break out in a cold sweat.

## I'm a shadow artist

Cameron spends part of [the first week](https://juliacameronlive.com/week/week-one-shadow-artists/ 'cite: Julia Cameron, Week One: Shadow Artists') on shadow artists: people who live close to art without making their own, often in careers right next to the dream they set down. That's me. I ended up in developer marketing, a creative job inside a technical field, and I've done well there.

I've always felt like a bit of an outsider in tech. After reading the book, I see that as one of the biggest strengths I bring to my work.

## Morning pages

[Morning pages](https://juliacameronlive.com/ 'cite: Julia Cameron, juliacameronlive.com') are three pages of longhand, stream-of-consciousness writing, every morning, about anything. They're the core of the book, and they're what I'm keeping. I think about the same stuff over and over, and dumping it onto the page lets me stop. I've written more about [what that does for my workday](/newsletter/taste-matters-more-with-ai/).

I write them every day. My partner and I do them together, which makes it easier, and reading the book with someone meant we could talk through each chapter and then write about it.

I couldn't find a study of morning pages themselves. The closest research is on expressive writing, where a [2006 meta-analysis of 146 studies](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17073523/ 'cite: Frattaroli, Psychological Bulletin, 2006') found a real but small average benefit. So I can't call them proven. I can only say they help me.

Plenty of people keep them up long after the book. Lexi Merritt has been [writing them for five years](https://leximerritt.substack.com/p/five-years-of-morning-pages 'cite: Lexi Merritt, Five years of morning pages | quote: These pages are the canon of my life.'). Here's Cameron explaining them herself:

[Tarcher Talks: Julia Cameron on morning pages](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EpDcrAVqIco)

## Artist dates

Artist dates got me back into poetry, drawing and sculpture parks. Cameron defines an [artist date](https://juliacameronlive.com/ 'cite: Julia Cameron, juliacameronlive.com') as a once-a-week solo outing to do something fun. I take them alone and with friends.

An artist date doesn't have to be a big outing. In my [Kiki's Delivery Service review](/reviews/kikis-delivery-service-1/) I wrote about Ursula telling Kiki what she does when she can't paint: walk, look at the scenery, nap, and wait for the urge to come back.

## What changed at work

Work has been so much better. The morning pages put me more in touch with what I feel, want and need, and I've never been this aware of what's going on with me. They've also made me more confident in my taste and in what I think needs to get done, and I've gotten better at saying it out loud.

The biggest change was realizing I didn't have to wait for permission to make something. [Connect AI × DOOM](/work/connect-ai-doom/) came straight out of that. It was an idea I'd already pitched at work, and it hadn't landed, but I knew it was there. So I built it, and it turned out to be a big hit.

![A hand holding a small round screen showing a green grid with glowing orange dots, like the Dragon Radar from Dragon Ball, with the words Thinking and Sending on it.](/images/blog/the-artists-way-review/dragon-ball-scanner.webp 'margin: My Dragon Ball scanner, November 2026.')

My [Dragon Ball scanner](https://www.instagram.com/p/DfnHYajt0yu/) came from the same place. It's a $50 ESP32 with custom firmware that animates it like the scanner from Dragon Ball, and it talks to my self-hosted Hermes server, which is hooked up to all my MCP servers at home. I can ask it which lights are on or what I need to do for work that day.

Little wins like that taught me that my ideas count. I think that needs saying, especially in the age of AI. What do I bring to the table that nobody else, and no computer, can? Being able to answer that out loud is going to be one of the most important skills for creative knowledge workers.

## Play at work

Artist dates are scheduled play, and play shouldn't need a productivity excuse. When I wrote [the cognitive case for play in technical work](/blog/cognitive-case-for-play-in-technical-work/), I framed it in technical terms on purpose so technical people would take it seriously. I wish they'd take it seriously on a more spiritual level, too.

![A black cat stretched out across a colorful oil pastel drawing of red flowers on a green gingham tablecloth.](/images/blog/the-artists-way-review/cat-on-my-painting.webp 'margin: A cat on one of my oil pastels.')

When I can be playful at work, my work is better. I'm in a creative job, and I have some successes under my belt that give me room to play. Not everyone has that room.

It's the same culture that made me stop making art in 2008, and it hasn't gone anywhere. Working in tech, and working in America, everything is stressful and everything is urgent for no reason. We're told to push and push without much thought beyond this quarter's earnings, which could always be higher. Hit a goal, and the goal gets reset so the urgency comes back. Stress for stress's sake stresses me out. It's part of the game, and I still don't understand it. Having to sell play as a productivity trick does it a massive disservice.

## The God language

The God language is my biggest gripe with the book, and it's the warning I give everyone who picks it up. Cameron was raised Catholic,[^scorsese] with [16 years of Catholic education](https://uscatholic.org/articles/202605/julia-cameron-believes-were-all-artists-and-mystics/ 'cite: U.S. Catholic, May 2026'), and the book frames creativity in those terms: your creativity is God's gift to you, and using it is your gift back to God. That's fine. It just never fully connected for me. I see it as a commitment to myself: to make things, and to stay connected to myself while I do.

My advice is to treat it the way AA treats a higher power: frame it in whatever light makes sense to you.

I do think there's something more universal in Cameron's idea. I like how David Lynch talks about creativity in [_Catching the Big Fish_](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/299829/catching-the-big-fish-by-david-lynch/ 'cite: David Lynch, Catching the Big Fish, 2006 | quote: Ideas are like fish.'): ideas come through you, and you have to pay close attention to catch them. He did that through Transcendental Meditation. Cameron does it through her walks and morning pages.

[David Lynch on where great ideas come from, The Atlantic](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mFsBaa_MEzM)

## I couldn't dream bigger

Dreaming bigger was the hardest part of the book for me, and I'm still struggling with it. The book keeps pushing you to do it, chapter after chapter.

I'm content with my beautiful little life here in Minnesota. I own a house and I'm stable. I have loving friends and people in my life who support me, and I have the means to go after my interests and passions. That's also why the book's money chapter, where [Defector's writers pushed back](https://defector.com/the-artists-way-week-6-are-we-feeling-abundant "cite: Defector, The Artist's Way week 6, 2025"), didn't bother me: I feel blessed with the money I make, and I live in St. Paul, an affordable part of the country.

When the book asked me to picture a bigger life, I had a hard time coming up with anything. It kept asking, so I watched [a movie about a painter in a SoHo loft](/reviews/new-york-stories/) to see what the big version looks like.

![Gru's Plan meme. Gru presents a plan: Do the morning pages. Go on artist dates. Dream bigger. In the last panel he stares at the board, which still says Dream bigger.](/images/blog/the-artists-way-review/meme-grus-plan.webp)

Part of why it's hard is my career. Sometimes it's felt like my career was dictated to me. Now I'm in a position to take control of it again and choose the direction I want to go, and that's going well. It still hasn't turned into a big dream.

I think Cameron is right to push. The hard part is what dreaming bigger means in our society: more responsibility, more hours, less time for yourself. I don't dream of that labor. Every time I think about a job that might be cool, I start thinking about all the hard work that comes with it, and that work doesn't bring me joy.

![Joe in a baseball cap outside at night, holding up a small plush toy wearing a shirt that says MOVIE FAN.](/images/blog/the-artists-way-review/movie-fan.webp 'margin: Me and a MOVIE FAN plush.')

I know a lot more about what I don't want. I love film and I love [writing about film](/reviews/), but I don't want to be a director or an actor. I don't want to be a CMO, a CEO or a CTO, and I don't want a huge amount of responsibility. I don't know if I want to be an author.

What I do want:

- Time and freedom.
- Low stress.
- To be financially stable.
- To be appreciated for my work.
- To collaborate with people who make me better.
- To make art, and to do my work with joy and for the love of the game.

Everything on that list is about how I work, not what I become.

## Is The Artist's Way worth it?

Yes, especially if you work in tech. You don't have to call yourself an artist. If you work in a creative field like marketing, or even programming, you'll get a lot out of it, and so will anybody else, at any stage of their life.

Programmers are creative people doing creative work, which is the case I made in my TEDx talk, [The Art of Computer Science](/talks/the-art-of-computer-science/). The book is one of those non-traditional ones that can help your career, because it helps you figure out where you thrive. For me that turned out to be how I work, more than what I do.

Not every part will land. A group of Defector writers did [the whole program together](https://defector.com/the-artists-way-week-12-is-julia-cameron-wrong 'cite: Chris Thompson, Defector, April 2025 | quote: Just let me live, Julia.') and complained plenty along the way, and [Ozy Brennan](https://thingofthings.substack.com/p/book-review-the-artists-way "cite: Ozy Brennan, Thing of Things, July 2025 | quote: the most annoying part about the Artist's Way is that it actually works"), who was annoyed by a lot of it, still admits it works.

Read it with somebody if you can, and do the morning pages even if you skip everything else.

## What's next

I'm starting Cameron's [_The Vein of Gold_](https://juliacameronlive.com/book/the-vein-of-gold/ 'cite: Julia Cameron, The Vein of Gold, 1996'), her 1996 follow-up, because I want to go deeper on my own art. I'm still trying to figure out what my dreams are, and every day I work and make something, I get a little closer to finding out.

I'd love to hear what reading _The Artist's Way_ was like for you and what it changed, especially if you're an engineer. Find me on [Bluesky](https://bsky.app/profile/joekarlsson.com) or [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/joekarlsson/).
